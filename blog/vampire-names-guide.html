Vampire Names: Gothic, Slavic Roots & Ancient Bloodlines

Published 14 August 2026

Build vampire names that fit the century your vampire died in — gothic and Victorian, Slavic and Romanian, ancient and modern.

Vampires present a naming problem that no other fantasy creature does: they were all somebody else first. An elf's name comes from elven culture, an orc's from orcish culture, but a vampire's name comes from whatever village, city or empire produced the mortal who died there. The name is a fossil. It records a place and a date, and everything about it should feel slightly out of step with the present day.

This is why "Kayden the Bloodlord" fails and "Constantin Vasilescu" works instantly. The first is a modern name wearing a cape; the second tells you the character was born somewhere in the Carpathians, probably in the eighteenth or nineteenth century, into a family with enough standing to have a surname worth keeping. All the atmosphere is doing its work before the fangs come out.

Step One: Decide When and Where They Died

Answer two questions before you write a single letter:

  • What year did they stop aging? A vampire turned in 1480 keeps a 1480 name. Naming fashions move fast — a name that was ordinary in 1650 sounds antique by 1900 and theatrical today.
  • What language did their mother speak? This decides the phonology, the surname pattern and the spelling conventions. It matters more than any aesthetic choice.

Then apply the drift rule: the older the vampire, the fewer name-parts they still use. Recently turned vampires keep a full modern name. Centuries-old ones shed the surname, then the given name, until only a title or a single word remains. That progression is a free characterisation tool — the party can guess a vampire's age from how they introduce themselves.

Gothic & Victorian Vampire Names

The classic register: Latinate given names that were fashionable among the European gentry from roughly 1700 to 1900, paired with surnames that sound like the family owns a house too large to heat. Reach for names with -us, -ian, -elle and heavy consonant clusters, and surnames drawn from estates, weather and stone.

Slavic & Romanian Roots

The vampire of folklore is at home in Eastern Europe long before it reached English literature — the Romanian strigoi and moroi, the Slavic upir. Names from this region carry the tradition honestly, and they have a real grammar you can use rather than imitate.

Slavic given names are frequently built from two meaning-elements: vlad (rule), mir (peace, world), slav (glory), rad (glad), bog (god), dragu (dear). Romanian surnames commonly take -escu ("descended from"); Slavic ones take -ovich/-evich, -ov, -sky or -ic.

Ancient Vampires

For a vampire who predates surnames entirely, drop the family name and reach for the naming stock of the classical and pre-classical world — Latin, Greek, Egyptian, Phoenician, Mesopotamian. These names are single words, often heavy with vowels, and they should sound faintly like something inscribed rather than spoken. Attaching an epithet in place of a surname is period-accurate and instantly ominous.

Modern Vampires & Working Aliases

A vampire who has survived into the present has learned that being memorable is a liability. Modern vampire names are deliberately plain: a common given name, an unremarkable surname, and paperwork that gets replaced every forty years. The horror is in the mismatch — an eight-hundred-year-old creature calling itself Dan Foster.

A useful trick is the initial carry: each new identity keeps the initials of the original name, so a Wilhelmina Sarre becomes W. Sanders, then Wendy Sable, then Will Sark. Players who notice the pattern feel clever, which is exactly what you want.

Titles & Epithets

Ancient vampires are usually named by their victims rather than by themselves. Epithets follow a simple grammar: the + a quality, an absence, or a place. Keep them concrete. "The Pale Duke of Aravin" beats "the Lord of Eternal Darkness" because it implies a map.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should a vampire keep their mortal name?

Most do, at least at first — it is the last piece of their old life. Vampires who deliberately discard the name are usually making a statement about what they have become, and that decision is worth a line of backstory.

Are apostrophes and umlauts a good idea?

Rarely. Vampire names draw power from being real historical names, and invented punctuation pushes them back toward generic fantasy. If you want strangeness, use an archaic spelling of a genuine name instead.

How do I name a vampire spawn or thrall?

Give them names from the local region and the current century — they were turned recently, so they should sound modern next to their master. The contrast makes the master's antiquity audible.

What about vampire family or bloodline names?

Treat a bloodline like a noble house: one surname, held for centuries, granted rather than inherited. Names such as House Corvane or the Vasilescu line let you signal politics without inventing a whole language.
