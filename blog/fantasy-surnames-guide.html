Fantasy Surnames: The Four Sources of a Family Name

Published 15 August 2026

Occupational, locational, patronymic, descriptive — where each really came from, how each reads on the page, and how to use surname style to signal class, region and race.

A given name tells you what a character's parents hoped for. A surname tells you what the world decided about their family — and it usually decided one of four things: what they did, where they were from, who their father was, or what they looked like. Nearly every European surname in existence falls into one of those four buckets, and once you can see the buckets you can build a naming system for an entire invented culture in about twenty minutes.

More usefully, the four sources do not feel the same. An occupational surname reads as working, a locational one as landed, a patronymic as traditional and a descriptive as familiar or unkind. Choosing between them is characterisation, not decoration.

Where Surnames Actually Came From

Hereditary surnames are more recent than most fantasy assumes. In England they spread gradually after the Norman Conquest — the aristocracy first, taking their names from the estates they held — and only became general across the population during the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries. Before that, a byname was a description attached to a living person, not a possession passed to their children. John the smith's son was not necessarily a Smith; he was John's son, or the miller, or the one with the red hair.

That transitional state is worth stealing wholesale. A world where surnames are still hardening is a world where a name can change within a lifetime, where a family's name records the moment they became notable, and where having an inherited surname at all is itself a class marker. Almost no fantasy setting uses this, and it costs nothing.

The four sources below are the standard classification used by surname scholarship, and each behaves differently once it lands in a story.

Source One: Occupational Surnames

The most transparent of the four: the family is named for a trade. Smith is the commonest surname in England precisely because every village needed one. Wright comes from Old English wyrhta, "maker" — and survives inside wheelwright, cartwright and shipwright. Fletcher is an arrow-maker, from Old French flèche. Cooper makes barrels; Chandler makes candles; Fuller cleans and thickens cloth, a job that produced the surnames Walker and Tucker in other parts of England for the identical trade. Chapman is a merchant, from Old English cēapmann, the root that also gives us "cheap".

For invented names, the grammar is simple: a noun plus an agent ending, or the bare tool. Keep the object concrete and the ending plain — -er, -wright, -man, -smith, or nothing at all.

Source Two: Locational Surnames

Locational surnames split into two kinds that read very differently.

Toponymic names come from a specific settlement — the family is from, or held, a named place. This is the Norman aristocratic pattern, originally expressed with de: de Clare, de Vere, de Montfort. These names are inherently grand, because owning a place and being named for it are the same fact.

Topographic names come from a landscape feature next to which the family lived: Hill, Brook, Ford, Wood, Marsh, Bridge, and the compressed prepositional forms like Underhill and Attwood ("at the wood"). These are humble by construction. Nobody owns a hill; they merely live below it.

The distinction is the single most efficient class signal in surname design. Rendric of Marchmont holds land. Rendric Underhill does not.

Source Three: Patronymic Surnames

The father's name, marked as such. This is the most linguistically varied of the four, because almost every language invented its own way of saying "son of":

  • English -son — Johnson, Wilson, Harrison.
  • Norman Fitz- — from Anglo-Norman fitz, ultimately Latin filius: Fitzgerald, Fitzwilliam.
  • Gaelic Mac / Mc — "son": MacDonald, McGregor.
  • Irish Ó — from ua, "grandson, descendant": O'Brien, O'Connor.
  • Welsh ap / ab — "son of", and it fused into the following name to produce a whole family of surnames: ap Hywel became Powell, ap Rhys became Price, ab Owain became Bowen, ap Richard became Pritchard.
  • Scandinavian -sen / -son — Andersen, Karlsson. In Iceland these are still true patronymics rather than family names, with -son and -dóttir formed fresh from each father's name in each generation.
  • Slavic -ovich / -evich, Hebrew ben, Armenian -ian.

Two things make patronymics valuable in fiction. First, they date a culture: a society still using live patronymics has not yet settled into hereditary surnames, which tells the reader something about its institutions. Second, they let a name carry a fact. If the party has heard of Aldric, then meeting Kel Aldricson is a plot point disguised as an introduction.

Source Four: Descriptive Surnames

The nickname that stuck. Descriptive surnames record a physical trait, a habit or a reputation, and they are the only one of the four sources that can be unkind. Brown, Black and White refer to hair or complexion. Reed and Read come from Old English rēad, "red", almost certainly of hair. Long, Little and Small are what they look like. Armstrong is a strong arm. Fairfax is Old English fæger feax, "fair hair". Russell derives from an Old French word for "little red one". Grant comes from Anglo-Norman graunt, "tall" or "great".

Note how many are about hair. Real descriptive bynames cluster tightly on the handful of features you can see across a market square — colouring, height, build, temper — because that is what distinguishes one John from another at fifty paces. Inventing descriptive surnames about someone's inner life is the commonest mistake here; nobody was ever nicknamed for being thoughtful.

How Each Source Reads on the Page

Same character, four surnames, four different people:

  • Kel Fletcher — competent, working, probably useful in a fight and definitely useful in a shop. Occupational names imply capability.
  • Kel Marchmont — somebody's heir, or pretending to be. Toponymic names imply property.
  • Kel Aldricson — traditional, tied to a father, from a culture with long memory. Patronymics imply lineage.
  • Kel Longshanks — known personally by whoever named him. Descriptive names imply familiarity, and often a slight lack of respect.

Which means you can change a character's apparent social position without touching a word of their description. It also means mixing sources within one family should be deliberate: a household containing a Marchmont and a Longshanks is telling you a story about a marriage, a bastard, or a fall.

Signalling Class with Surname Style

Four dials, all of them cheap to turn:

  • Length. High status runs long and polysyllabic; low status runs short and blunt. Vandermere against Skeg.
  • Particles. de, von, of, ap — anything that grammatically marks a relationship to land or lineage reads as aristocratic, because in reality it required having land or a lineage worth marking.
  • Transparency. The lower the status, the more legible the name. A peasant is called Ford because he lives by the ford. A duke is called Corveth because four hundred years ago somebody's ancestor held a valley whose name has since changed.
  • Stability. Noble surnames are fixed and defended. Poor surnames drift — the same family may be recorded as Attwood, Atwood and Wood across three generations, and a scribe's spelling is the only authority.

Signalling Region with Surname Style

Surnames cluster geographically in reality, and readers register the clustering as culture. Give each region of your world a dominant source and one characteristic sound, then let the borders blur.

  • The old farming heartland — occupational and topographic, soft consonants, everything two syllables.
  • The northern coast — patronymic, because the settlers who arrived by sea brought the habit with them. Hard consonants, -son and -dottir.
  • The court and its counties — toponymic with particles, borrowed vocabulary, deliberately foreign-sounding because the ruling class arrived as conquerors.
  • The hill country — descriptive, short, occasionally rude, and largely unchanged for centuries because nobody up there writes anything down.

When Fantasy Races Shouldn't Have Surnames

Surnames exist to solve a bureaucratic problem: too many people with the same given name in a system that needs to tell them apart for taxation, inheritance and law. If a culture does not have that problem, it should not have surnames — and refusing to give a race surnames is one of the strongest ways to make it feel genuinely non-human.

  • Small, scattered populations don't need them. A community of two hundred that never sees a tax collector manages fine with one name and a nickname.
  • Very long-lived peoples may not use them. If an elf's given name is unique in living memory and she will use it for six centuries, a family name adds nothing. Elves in most settings take a house or lineage name instead, which is a different thing: a political affiliation, not an identifier.
  • Clan cultures replace them. Dwarves famously carry a clan name that behaves like a surname but is not one — it is inherited from a much larger group and says nothing about the immediate family. The dwarf naming guide goes into how clan and craft names divide that work.
  • Some peoples stack instead. Gnomes accumulate given names, earned nicknames and clan names simultaneously, which is a fundamentally different structure from one-given-plus-one-family; the gnome naming guide lays out the whole stack.
  • Monstrous and tribal cultures use epithets. Orcs, goblins and their kin are typically given one name plus a deed. That is closer to how bynames worked before they became hereditary, and it is the older system, not a cruder one.

The rule of thumb: give a people surnames when their society writes things down about them. Literacy, taxation and land law are what produce family names, so the presence of a surname is a statement about the state, not about the family.

Building a Surname System for Your World

Twenty minutes, five steps:

  1. Decide when surnames became hereditary in your world, or whether they have yet. Everything else follows from that date.
  2. Assign a dominant source to each region — one of the four, not a mixture. Mixtures come later, from migration.
  3. Write ten first elements per region: ten trades, ten landscape words, ten father-names, ten traits. That is forty words and it will generate hundreds of names.
  4. Set the class dials: what marks a noble name here, and what marks a poor one? Write one example of each so you can hear the gap.
  5. Plant three anomalies. A patronymic family in the court counties. A de name in a fishing village. A trade surname for a trade that no longer exists. Each one is a story someone can ask about.

Then stop designing and start using. If you need a batch of family names to fill out a village roster or a noble house, the family name generator builds from all four sources, and the NPC naming guide covers how to deploy them at speed once play begins.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should every character in my setting have a surname?▼
No, and the exceptions are useful. In a world where surnames are still spreading, having one marks you as someone the authorities have recorded. A character with only a given name is poor, foreign, illegitimate or hiding — all four of which are more interesting than a default surname.
Can one family use two different surname styles?▼
Yes, and it usually means something. Historically a family might be recorded by trade in one generation and by place in the next, depending on who was writing. In fiction, treat the switch as evidence: a branch that moved, married up, or wanted to be forgotten.
Do elves need family names?▼
Rarely. Long life plus small populations plus unique given names removes the problem surnames solve. Most settings give elves a house or lineage name instead, which functions as an allegiance rather than an identifier — and that difference is worth playing up.
How do I invent surnames that don't sound modern?▼
Build them from objects and jobs that no longer exist, and avoid abstractions entirely. Nobody was surnamed for a concept. Reach for tools, animals, weather, body parts and landscape features, then say the result aloud — if it sounds like a word rather than a brand, it will pass.
