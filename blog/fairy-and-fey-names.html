Fairy & Fey Names: Seelie, Unseelie and Nature Naming

True names and use-names, court sound palettes, nature-derived compounds, and 75+ examples for pixies, sprites, dryads and archfey.

Fey names fail in one of two directions. Aim too sweet and you get a Tinkerbell — a name so twee that nobody at the table can take the character seriously as a threat or a power. Aim too grim and you have simply written a demon with wings. The fey of folklore live in the tension between those extremes: beautiful, delighted, playful, and entirely willing to trade a child for a bowl of cream. The name has to hold both.

The other complication is unique to the fey. In almost every version of the legend, a name is not a label but a handle — knowing a fey's true name gives you leverage over it. That single belief reshapes the entire naming system, because it means fey rarely tell anyone what they are actually called.

The Three Layers of a Fey Name

  • The true name — spoken once at the moment of a fey's making and guarded ever after. It should feel unpronounceable and slightly wrong: long, vowel-heavy, full of sounds that don't sit comfortably in a mortal mouth. Most players will never hear it, which is exactly why it should exist.
  • The use-name — the working name. Almost always a compound of natural things: a plant plus a quality, a weather word plus a body part. This is what the character is called in play.
  • The court title — an office or an epithet earned within a court: the Knight of Thistles, the Lady of the Second Frost. Titles are the currency of fey politics and often replace the use-name entirely at formal occasions.

The practical upshot: a fey who gives you a name has given you almost nothing, and a fey who asks for yours is making a move. Some of the best fey encounters at a table are built entirely on that exchange.

Seelie Court Names

The Seelie — the "blessed" court of summer and light in the Scottish and Irish tradition — are not kind so much as bound by courtesy. Their names should sound warm and musical, but never simple. Build them from:

  • Liquid consonants (l, r, n, m) and soft f, v, s
  • Open vowels and vowel pairs — ae, ia, eo, ui
  • Endings in -wyn, -iel, -ara, -lys, -ine, -ora
  • Imagery of dawn, gold, honey, blossom, running water and high summer

Unseelie Court Names

The Unseelie are the winter half — the court of the hunt, the hollow hill and the long night. Their names use the same grammar as Seelie names but a colder palette. Do not simply make them ugly; a well-made Unseelie name should still be beautiful, in the way that frost on a window is beautiful.

  • Sharper onsets: k, sk, th, gr, bl, vr
  • Clipped or hissing endings: -ith, -esk, -orn, -ash, -rix, -vell
  • Imagery of thorn, frost, hollow, ash, smoke, briar, moth and the moon's dark half

Pixies, Sprites & Small Fey

Small fey are where the diminutive register belongs — and where it works. The trick that keeps these names from becoming cloying is the sting in the tail: pair a sweet element with something faintly menacing, so the name is funny right up until it isn't. Pib Nettlewick sounds adorable. Then you learn what Nettlewick does.

Nature-Derived Use-Names

This is the most reliable formula in fey naming, and it works for every court and every size of fey. Take one natural noun and bolt it to a second word that describes an action, a texture or a time. Do not hyphenate. Do not explain.

  • Plants: thistle, briar, moss, fern, alder, rowan, elder, nettle, willow, hazel
  • Weather & light: dew, frost, gloam, mist, ember, glimmer, thunder, shade
  • Second elements: -down, -veil, -wick, -fall, -song, -whistle, -crown, -mantle, -hollow, -bough

Archfey Titles

The greatest fey are known almost entirely by title. The pattern is simple and enormously effective: a definite article, a rank or role, and a domain that sounds specific rather than cosmic. "The Queen of Air and Darkness" works; "the Fairy Queen" does not.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should my fey character have a true name in play?

Yes — write it down, hand it to the DM, and never say it aloud. It costs nothing and gives your table a ready-made plot device. Make it long and awkward on purpose.

Are Seelie names always pretty and Unseelie names always harsh?

No, and treating it as a rule makes both courts boring. The courts differ in temperament and law, not in beauty — an Unseelie noble may have the loveliest name at the ball. Use the sound palette as a lean, not a law.

What about firbolgs, satyrs, dryads and other fey creatures?

The nature-compound formula covers all of them. Dryads take their name from their tree; satyrs favour short, wine-warm names with Greek endings; firbolgs use long descriptive phrases translated into plain speech.

Can a fey lie about its name?

In most traditions a fey cannot lie outright — but it can give a use-name, a title, or a name that belongs to somebody else, all while telling the literal truth. That distinction is the whole game.
