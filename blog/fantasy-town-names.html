Fantasy Town Names: Villages, Ports & Cities That Sound Real

Published 15 August 2026

What the old English suffixes actually meant, how settlement size changes a name, and how to keep seventy places on one map sounding like one country.

Almost nobody deliberately named a village. Villages were described — by the people walking past them, in the language they happened to speak — and the description hardened into a name once everyone forgot what the words meant. That is the single most useful fact in place-name invention, because it tells you what a convincing town name is: a plain sentence about geography, ownership or trade, worn smooth by four hundred years of mumbling.

The names that fail on fantasy maps fail because they were designed rather than described. Shadowspire, Ravenmoor and Grimhaven read as marketing. They tell you how the author feels about the place instead of what a farmer standing in a field would have called it. Meanwhile Ashford, Nantwich and Buttermere — all real — sound instantly plausible because each is only a noun and a landscape feature stuck together.

Why Real Place Names Feel Real

Three properties do nearly all the work, and none of them is exotic:

  • They are boring on purpose. The reference is usually a ford, a hill, a clearing, a bridge, a lake or somebody's name. A place called Oxford is a place where oxen crossed a river, and that is the whole story.
  • The meaning is half-buried. An English speaker hears "Oxford" as a unit before they hear "ox" plus "ford". That slight opacity is what stops a name reading as a label. If your reader can decode the whole thing at a glance, it feels invented.
  • They cluster. Real place names come in families, because a region's names were laid down by one language in one era. Suffixes repeat across a district the way surnames repeat across a village.

Get those three right and you barely need imagination. You need a wordlist and a map.

The Etymology of English Place-Name Suffixes

These are the workhorses. The etymologies below are the standard ones given by place-name scholarship — knowing what each element genuinely meant lets you use it correctly rather than decoratively, which is exactly what separates a map that reads as a country from a map that reads as a list.

  • -ton — from Old English tūn, originally "enclosure" or "fenced yard", then "farmstead", then "estate", and finally "village or town". It is cognate with German Zaun (fence) and Dutch tuin (garden). It is the most common English place-name ending by a wide margin, so use it for ordinary inland settlements — the ones nobody writes songs about.
  • -wick / -wich — from Old English wīc, itself borrowed from Latin vīcus, "village" or "dwelling place". In practice it very often marks a specialised settlement: a dairy farm, an outlying dependent site, or a trading post. In the salt districts of Cheshire and Worcestershire the -wich towns (Nantwich, Northwich, Droitwich) are associated specifically with saltworks. Use it for places that exist to make or trade one thing.
  • -ford — Old English ford, a shallow river crossing. Utterly literal, and enormously useful, because a ford explains why a settlement exists at all: people stopped there because the water did not. Every -ford on your map is a road decision.
  • -bury / -borough / -burgh — from Old English burh, "fortified place, stronghold" (the -bury form comes from its dative byrig). Related to German -burg. This is a defended site: a hillfort, a walled town, a burh of the sort Alfred's England built against Viking raids. Never hang -bury on a hamlet — the element is a promise of walls.
  • -combe — from Old English cumb, "short steep valley" or "hollow", generally taken as an early borrowing from a Brittonic Celtic word related to Welsh cwm. It clusters in the south-west of England, and it is one of the best elements you have for signalling hill country in a single syllable.
  • -thorpe — Old Norse þorp, a secondary or outlying settlement: the daughter farm founded by people from the older village down the road. It is concentrated in the Danelaw — Yorkshire, Lincolnshire, the east Midlands — which makes it perfect for marking the region your invaders settled.
  • -mere — Old English mere, "lake, pool, standing water". Windermere and Grasmere are the famous survivals. A -mere name puts flat water next to the settlement and quietly promises fish, reeds, mist and a reason not to build a road.

Notice how much information those seven carry. A traveller told they are heading for Saltwick, then Coldford, then Amberbury already knows they will pass a works, a crossing and a wall, in that order.

More Elements Worth Stealing

Seven suffixes will make a region feel samey. These extend the palette, and again the meanings are the real ones:

  • -by — Old Norse býr, "farmstead, village". Also Danelaw: Grimsby, Whitby, Derby.
  • -ham — Old English hām, "homestead, village". Genuinely hard to tell apart from hamm, "enclosure" or "land in a river bend"; scholars often cannot separate them without early spellings, which is a nice licence for you.
  • -ley / -leigh — Old English lēah, "woodland clearing, glade". Forest country.
  • -don / -dun — Old English dūn, "hill, down".
  • -worth — Old English worþ, "enclosure, homestead".
  • -stead — Old English stede, "place, site".
  • -stow — Old English stōw, "place", frequently a holy place or an assembly place. Excellent for shrines and moot-sites.
  • -caster / -chester — Old English ceaster, from Latin castra, "camp". These mark old Roman forts, so in a fantasy setting they mark the ruins of whatever empire came before yours. This is the single most efficient way to imply a fallen civilisation on a map.
  • -mouth — the mouth of the named river: Plymouth, Yarmouth, Exmouth.
  • -wold / -weald — Old English weald, high forest land.

Settlement Size Changes the Name

This is the rule most maps break. Size is not just a number on a key; it changes who did the naming and therefore what the name is made of.

  • Hamlets are named by neighbours. Two or three words, entirely descriptive, often with a qualifier bolted on to distinguish it from the bigger place nearby: Little, Nether, Upper, Cold, Old. Nobody outside a day's walk has heard the name.
  • Villages are named by their district. One compound, one landscape feature, one suffix. They may carry a family name if a single household owned the land.
  • Towns get qualified. Once a place matters enough to be confused with another, it acquires a distinguishing tag: a river (Marden on Wey), a saint, a lord's family, or a Latin-ish size marker of the sort that gives real England its Magna and Parva pairs.
  • Cities lose their meaning fastest. Heavy use erodes a name. Big places contract, drop syllables and end up as compact, slightly opaque units — which is why great cities so often have short names.
  • Capitals get renamed. Somebody powerful arrived and imposed a name, so capitals break the descriptive rule on purpose. That break is the point: it says a king was here.

Port and Harbour Names

Ports are named by sailors, and sailors name for navigation and for weather. A port name should be shoutable from a deck and should tell an approaching ship something useful: what the headland looks like, what the anchorage is worth, what the place smells of. Salt, tar, rope, gulls, kelp and shelter all belong here.

Saltmouth
Hookport
Gullwick
Brine Haven
Coldharbour
Mastford
Tarnmouth
Whalewick
Netherstrand
Kelpmere
Rope Quay
Anchorbury

City Names

Cities have been said aloud too many times to keep their full shape. Contract them. Drop a syllable, fuse the elements, let the vowels blur. Ashburnham becomes Asham; Duskingford becomes Duskford. The result should be short, slightly hard to parse, and comfortable in a sentence like "he took ship for ___ in the spring".

Highbury
Duskford
Corvane
Ashenbury
Greatmere
Threeford
Marrowbury
Vaultmere
Kingsford
Wenchester

Naming a Capital

A capital is the one place on your map that is allowed to sound designed, because it was. Someone with an army decided what it would be called, and their choice was political. Pick one of four motives and commit to it:

  • The founder. A personal name plus a settlement element, or the founder's name barely disguised. This is the commonest real pattern for imposed names.
  • The dynasty. The ruling house's name used as a root, so every subject says the family's name daily without noticing.
  • The abstract virtue. Sanctuary, concord, dominion, dawn. This is the register of an empire that thinks well of itself, and it reads very differently from a farmer's description.
  • The older language. The capital keeps a name in the tongue of whoever was there first, because conquerors love the legitimacy of an ancient word. This works beautifully alongside your -caster ruins.
Aureth
Kaldenbury
Marrowhold
Corvane Royal
Sanctus Vale
Highthrone
Valdemere
Emperor's Ford
Adamant
Thessary

The contrast is the whole trick. Put Aureth and Cold Combe in the same kingdom and the reader instantly understands the distance between the palace and the parish. If you are naming the realm around that capital too, the kingdom naming guide covers how the two should relate.

Regional Consistency on the Map

Here is the discipline that turns a name list into a country. Divide your map into language zones — three is plenty — and assign each zone a small kit: two or three suffixes, a handful of characteristic first elements, and one sound it never uses.

  • Zone one, the old heartland: -ton, -ford, -ley. Soft, agricultural, unremarkable.
  • Zone two, the settled coast: -thorpe, -by, -wick. Harder consonants, because the invaders spoke a different language and their words stayed.
  • Zone three, the hill country: -combe, -mere, -dun. Older still, borrowed from the people who were there before anyone wrote anything down.

Then obey your own zones. A single -thorpe stranded in the hill country is not a mistake to be corrected — it is a story about a family who moved, and one such anomaly per map is a gift. Two dozen of them is just noise.

The second half of consistency is repetition of first elements, not just suffixes. Real districts reuse roots: a Marden, a Marling, a Marnham and a Mardenmere within thirty miles of each other. Readers register that echo as authenticity long before they could explain why.

A Worked Example: One River Valley

Take a river called the Wey, running from hill country down to the sea. Walk it from source to mouth and name what you pass, letting the zone kit change as the land does:

Weyhead Combe
Upper Weyley
Weyford
Netherwey
Weybury
Weythorpe
Saltwey
Weymouth Quay

Eight names, one root, and a reader can now trace the whole watercourse without a map — up in the hollow at the top, a clearing, a crossing, the low ground, the fortified market, a later Norse daughter-settlement, the saltworks and finally the harbour. That is roughly forty seconds of work and it does more for a setting than a page of description. Frequently Asked Questions

Can I just use real English suffixes in a fantasy world?▼
Yes, and most published fantasy does. The suffixes read as generically old rather than specifically English to nearly every reader. The risk is only in the first elements: pair a real suffix with an obviously modern or joke root and the illusion collapses.
How many towns should I name before play starts?▼
Name the capital, the two or three settlements your players will actually reach, and keep a spare list of eight. Names invented at the table under pressure are reliably worse than names picked calmly, and a spare list costs you five minutes.
Should a fantasy city name mean something?▼
It should have meant something, once. The ideal is a name whose meaning a scholar in your world could explain but an ordinary citizen has never thought about. That half-forgotten quality is what real city names have and invented ones usually lack.
How do I stop every town on my map sounding the same?▼
Vary the suffix by region rather than at random, and vary the number of syllables. If eleven of your fifteen settlements are two-syllable compounds ending in a landscape word, break the pattern with a short contracted city, a qualified hamlet and one name in an older language.
