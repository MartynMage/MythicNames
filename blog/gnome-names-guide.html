Gnome Names: The Multi-Name Tradition & Tinker Naming

Given names, earned nicknames, clan names and the famous gnome introduction — with 80+ examples for rock, forest and tinker gnomes.

Most fantasy races have one name too few. Gnomes have several too many. Where a dwarf gets a given name and a clan name and considers the matter settled, a gnome accumulates names the way a workshop accumulates half-finished projects — a name from their parents, a name from their clan, a name their neighbours started using and never stopped, a name earned by one memorable disaster, and a shorter thing they answer to at the market. All of them are real. None of them is redundant.

This is what makes gnome names genuinely tricky to write. The problem is not finding a name that sounds gnomish; the problem is deciding which of a gnome's many names your character actually uses, in front of whom, and what it says about them that they chose that one.

The Multi-Name Tradition

In the standard D&D presentation of gnomes, a gnome's full name is a stack of separate names, each with a different source and a different social function:

  • The given name — chosen by the parents, usually bouncy and multi-syllabic. Used by family and on formal documents.
  • The nickname — earned, not given. It records a deed, a habit or an accident, and it sticks whether or not the gnome likes it.
  • The clan or family name — the inherited one, typically a compound of two plain words. Gnome clans are extended and sprawling, so this places a gnome in a network rather than a household.
  • Neighbour names — additional names granted informally by the community. A gnome may collect several over a long life, and a very sociable gnome may collect a dozen.
  • The short name — what the gnome actually says when a human asks, because humans have short memories and shorter patience.

The comedy — and the characterisation — lives in the gap between the full name and the short one. A gnome who leads with the whole stack is either very proud or deliberately wasting your time; a gnome who offers only one syllable is hiding something, or has simply met humans before.

Gnome Phonology: What Makes a Name Sound Gnomish

Gnome names are built for cheerfulness, and the rules behind that effect are surprisingly specific:

  • Front-load the stress. Gnome names are trochaic — FID-dle-wick, ZOOK-buh-tee. The bounce comes from a strong first syllable followed by weak ones.
  • Use plosives and voiced stops — b, d, g, p, k — and lots of them. Gnome names pop.
  • Double your consonants in the middle: Fibb-, Nikk-, Gimm-, Widd-.
  • Reach for diminutive endings — -ik, -in, -bin, -kin, -dle, -ble, -wick, -et, -ip, -us.
  • Three syllables is the sweet spot for a given name. Two feels curt; four starts to feel like a clan name.
  • Avoid grandeur. No apostrophes, no -ath or -oth endings, no dark vowels. A gnome name should be impossible to say ominously.

Tinker Surnames

The tinker register is the most fun to build and the easiest to overdo. The formula is mechanism + action or noise. Pick one word from a workshop and one word that describes what it does when it goes wrong, and resist the urge to use more than two.

  • First elements: sprocket, cog, gear, spring, bellows, flint, brass, copper, piston, ratchet, wrench, spindle
  • Second elements: -whistle, -whittle, -spanner, -clatter, -fizzle, -bang, -tumble, -crank, -sputter, -gadget, -tinker, -snap

Rock, Forest & Deep Gnomes

The subrace should tilt the sound palette:

  • Rock gnomes — the tinkers. Machine-shop surnames, clanging consonants, the longest full names.
  • Forest gnomes — quieter and more secretive. Softer given names and nature-word clan names: Fernwhistle, Mosslip, Burrowdown, Quillfeather.
  • Deep gnomes (svirfneblin) — shorter, harder, stripped of bounce. One or two syllables, grinding consonants, no diminutives at all: Grix, Dolt, Kraz, Vurn, Belwar, Schneeble. The absence of the usual gnomish cheer is itself the characterisation.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many names does a gnome actually need?

Three is plenty for play: a given name, a nickname and a clan name. The wider collection of neighbour names is a lovely detail to mention once and then quietly forget.

Do gnomes tell non-gnomes their full name?

Usually not — they trim it down for convenience, or because they have learned that most people stop listening after the second name. Which part they keep is a characterisation choice worth making deliberately.

Can a gnome choose their own nickname?

They can try. It rarely takes. Nicknames are conferred by the community, and a gnome who insists on a grander one than the one they earned is a very good running joke.

Are gnome names always comedic?

No. A deep gnome's name is flatly grim, a forest gnome's is quiet and green, and an elderly artificer's clan name can carry real weight. The bouncy tinker style is the loudest gnome register, not the only one.
